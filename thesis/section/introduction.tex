\section{Introduction}
When a clinical team considers adjusting the dosage of a life-saving therapy, when climate scientists debate geoengineering maneuvers to cool a rapidly warming region, or when an engineer tunes a cyber-physical system to avoid cascading failures, the decision at stake is inherently causal. In each of these arenas, we must hypothesize, intervene, and \emph{discover} how the world responds. \game was born from the observation that future AI systems—whether they triage patients, regulate energy grids, or design educational interventions—will only be trustworthy once they demonstrate mastery over this active experimentation loop.

\paragraph{Context and motivation.}
Modern causal inference has delivered powerful estimators for medicine, from adaptive treatment planning for sepsis and oncology \citep{Shi2022LearningCEA,Smith2023ApplicationOTA,Prosperi2020CausalIAA} to identification of heterogeneous effects in precision therapies \citep{Cai2017IdentificationOAA}. In the natural sciences, causal tools underpin discovery in materials science and climate modeling, where researchers reason over interdependent processes and noisy observational records \citep{Varghese2024CausalDTA,Zucker2021LeveragingSBA,runge2019causality}. Engineering disciplines rely on structural modeling to safeguard complex infrastructures—autonomous traffic management, smart grids, or chemical processes demand interpretable cause–effect maps that survive distribution shift \citep{Chadbourne2023ApplicationsOCA,Li2024InferringHTA,Graham2022CausalIFA}. Economists continue to probe policy interventions using counterfactual reasoning \citep{Athey2016TheSOA,lalonde1986evaluating,Abbring2025PhilipGWA}, while education scientists deploy causal uplift modeling to personalize tutoring at scale \citep{Nagai2024EducationalEIA,Chi2022APGA,weissman2021methods}. Yet practitioners in each community recount the same narrative: static datasets are insufficient; decisive insights emerge only once we can plan, execute, and interpret interventions under tight resource and ethical constraints.

\paragraph{Problem statement and gap.}
Despite a decade of progress in \emph{static} causal estimation—where a learner consumes a passively collected dataset to infer causal graphs or treatment effects \citep{johansson2016learning,shalit2017estimating,alaa2017bayesian,yoon2018ganite,zheng2018dags,kalainathan2022structural,lippe2021efficient,Cranmer2020DiscoveringSMA,Biggio2021NeuralSRA}—the field lacks a benchmark that evaluates how an autonomous agent \emph{chooses} experiments. Most established suites for graph discovery, symbolic regression, or uplift modeling \citep{chengcausaltime,lawrence2021data,chevalley2023causalbench,olson2017pmlb,la2021contemporary,romano2021pmlb,CauseBox2021,ACCBench2024,CIPCaDBench2022,CausalDiscoveryFramework2024,RealCause2021,CausalBench2024,EvaluationFramework2022,IBM2022,DoWhy2021,Framework2021} assume the data-collection strategy is external and immutable. Even CausalWorld, one of the few interactive environments, remains rooted in robotic control and does not measure the correctness of causal reasoning itself \citep{ahmed2020causalworld}. Consequently, we lack a principled way to compare agents that must balance curiosity, cost, and accuracy as they interrogate a causal system.

\paragraph{Objectives.}
The overarching objective of this thesis is to position causal inference as a resource-aware, agent-driven experimentation process. Concretely, we aim to (i) define a formal game that captures the sequential nature of causal discovery under budget constraints, (ii) instantiate that game in software so researchers can build and evaluate diverse agents, and (iii) demonstrate, through missions grounded in data-derived, Bayesian network, and physics-based SCMs, how interactive evaluation surfaces strengths and weaknesses that static benchmarks cannot reveal.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
To close the gap, we introduce \game, a turn-based, agent-centric causal inference benchmark in which players actively select interventions, gather observations, and decide when to stop and return a conclusion. Building on the observation of \citet{BayesianCausalDiscovery2023} that the separation between causal graph discovery and treatment effect estimation is largely artificial, \game treats both as \emph{missions} within a unified interface. Unlike static benchmarks, \game jointly evaluates (i) the quality of an agent’s causal conclusions and (ii) the efficiency with which those conclusions are earned. The accompanying Python package \package provides:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a formalized \emph{agent-based causal inference game} that scores not only \emph{what} an agent concludes but \emph{how intelligently} it explores;
    \item a modular \emph{system architecture} with declarative Specs, allowing researchers to plug in SCM generators, missions, metrics, and agents without altering the runtime;
    \item a principled evaluation protocol spanning DAG recovery, treatment-effect estimation, SCM reconstruction, and future mission types;
    \item fully seedable execution and artifact generation that guarantee fair, reproducible, and comparable assessment of heterogeneous causal agents.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Scope.}
This thesis focuses on turn-based experimentation over finite SCMs with explicit access modes (latent, observable, controllable variables). We deliberately exclude temporal partially observable processes, domain-specific reward shaping, and agent-learning algorithms that require privileged information beyond the game interface. Our scope instead emphasizes the general benchmarking layer that any community member can reuse or extend.

\paragraph{Significance and expected impact.}
By reframing causal inference as a strategic experimental process rather than a passive estimation task, \game enables evaluation of the next generation of \emph{interactive causal agents}—systems capable of planning interventions, quantifying uncertainty, and justifying when to stop. We expect the framework to catalyze rigorous competitions, accelerate the diagnostics of large reasoning models that claim causal understanding, and ultimately bridge the gap between academic algorithm design and the operational realities faced by scientists, engineers, and policymakers.
